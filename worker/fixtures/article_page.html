Why mangroves matter

Mangrove forests grow along tropical coastlines where salt water meets fresh water. Their tangled roots slow the tide, trap sediment and shelter young fish, crabs and shrimp. When storms arrive, a wide belt of mangroves can absorb much of the energy of the waves before they reach the shore.

Because their soils hold so much carbon, mangroves also store far more of it per hectare than most inland forests. Clearing them for shrimp ponds or building land releases that carbon and leaves the coast exposed to erosion.

Restoration works best when the original flow of tidal water is brought back first, so that seedlings can take root on their own instead of being planted in places where they cannot survive.
